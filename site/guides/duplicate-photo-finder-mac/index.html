How to find and remove duplicate photos on Mac — without regrets

Updated August 2026

Duplicate photos accumulate for boring reasons: the same shot backed up from two devices, an export from an old app, a burst you never culled, a RAW+JPEG pair from your camera. They waste real storage. But the tools that clean them up vary enormously in how much they protect you when something goes wrong — and with photos, "something goes wrong" means a memory is gone.

Exact duplicates vs. look-alikes

There are two different problems, and good tools treat them differently.

Exact duplicates are byte-identical files with different names or locations. These can be found with certainty by comparing file content (hashing) — filename comparison is worthless, since every camera produces its own IMG_0042.jpg. Removing exact duplicates is low-risk if the match is by content.

Near-duplicates are photos that look similar but aren't identical: burst sequences, RAW+JPEG pairs, a photo and its re-encoded copy, Live Photos and their stills. No algorithm should auto-delete these. The only safe workflow is side-by-side review where you pick the keeper.

The safety checklist for any duplicate cleaner

Before pointing a cleanup tool at your photo library, check:

  • Content-based matching. Duplicates found by file content, not name or size.
  • Preview before removal. You see the full list of what will be removed — and what will be kept — before anything happens.
  • Trash, not permanent delete. Removals go to the macOS Trash so you have weeks to change your mind. Tools that hard-delete for "efficiency" are optimizing the wrong thing.
  • Keeper logic you can see. When a tool suggests which copy to keep, you should be able to see why, and override it.
  • Pairs stay together. RAW+JPEG and Live Photo pairs should be handled as pairs, not split by an algorithm that doesn't know they're related.
  • A record of what happened. A log or receipt of every removal, so you can audit — or undo.

Before you start: one honest warning

Run duplicate cleanup after your photos are consolidated in one place, not while they're spread across five drives — otherwise today's "duplicate" is tomorrow's only surviving copy of a photo whose other instance lived on a drive that failed.
